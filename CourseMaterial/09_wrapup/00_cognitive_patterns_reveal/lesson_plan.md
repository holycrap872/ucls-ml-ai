## Essential Questions

- How do the patterns we've been spotting all year apply to ourselves?
- What does it mean to reason against your own preferences?

## Lesson Plan

This mini-unit (2-3 classes) is the "inward turn" of the cognitive patterns
thread. Students have spent the year naming biases in ChatBot output, article
arguments, and hypothetical 9th graders. Now they discover that the same
patterns apply to their own thinking. The reveal should feel earned — a
recognition of a skill developed, not a gotcha.

> Note: The candle problem is the catalyst. Both variants (matches in matchbox
  vs. matches loose) run as a manipulation across the class. The debrief
  connects the felt experience of a cognitive blind spot to the patterns
  students have been naming all term.

### Setup

- Candle problem materials (candle, box of tacks, matches in matchbox for half
  the class, matches loose for the other half)
- Grade prediction scatter plots printed for each student (compiled from the
  year's worth of predictions)
- One piece of past-self writing per student (from Unit 7 research paper)
- `cognitive_patterns_reference.md` projected

### Actual Lesson

- Candle problem (~20m)
    - Split class — half get matches in the matchbox, half get matches loose
    - Give 5m to solve
    - Reveal the manipulation: framing changed your performance
    - Debrief: "Your mind has invisible architecture, and that architecture is
      manipulable by framing. You just experienced it."
    - Name this as **functional fixedness** — a different kind of blind spot
      than the ones on our reference page. Not motivated reasoning, but still
      invisible architecture that shapes your thinking without your awareness.
    - Sit with that for a moment. Don't rush to connect it to the reference page yet.
- Transition (~5m)
    - "We've spent the year naming patterns in other people's thinking — bots,
      authors, hypothetical 9th graders. The candle problem just showed you a
      blind spot in your own thinking that had nothing to do with motivation.
      Now let's look at the ones that do."
- Pull threads together (~20m)
    - Hand back grade prediction scatter plots
        - What patterns do you see in your own calibration?
        - Were you consistently over- or under-confident?
        - Did your calibration improve over the year?
    - Walk through specific moments from the course where the patterns appeared
        - Agreement bias in ChatBot interactions
        - Automation bias in trusting CI/CD or AI suggestions
        - The pre-mortems — did your imagined failures match actual ones?
- Closing reflection (~10m)
    - The vocabulary you sharpened on the bot, on authors, on hypothetical 9th
      graders — it applies to you
    - Frame as a skill developed, not a flaw revealed
    - Connect to AI: the systems you will use for the rest of your lives are
      tuned to exploit these exact heuristics. The defense is calibration, not
      suspicion.
    - Hand out written takeaway (on paper):
        - "Good prompting, good reading, good teaching, and good engineering all
          require the same move: deliberately seeking the disconfirming view."

### Homework

- Write one sentence: what surprised you today? Bring it to next class.
